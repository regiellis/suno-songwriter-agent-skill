# Songwriting Craft

Use for lyric drafting and revision, hooks, dramatic movement, prosody, rhyme, and singability. For genre-specific writing decisions, load the relevant family from `genre-songwriting.md`. For notes and chords use `harmony-and-melody.md`; for meter and parts use `rhythm-and-arrangement.md`. Music craft is not a claim about how Suno internally works.

## Find The Song's Engine

Identify what makes the listener stay: a story unfolding, a revealing voice, an irresistible refrain, an argument, a riff, a groove, or a changing texture. Choose the form around that engine. A dance mantra need not tell a story; a ballad need not have a chorus; an image-led lyric need not resolve its ambiguity.

For a lyric-led song, privately establish:

- Speaker: who is talking, to whom, and from what immediate situation?
- Want: what do they want the listener or another character to do, believe, or understand?
- Friction: what makes the want difficult, embarrassing, costly, or contradictory?
- Hook: which phrase, rhythmic gesture, or image carries the song's identity?
- Movement: what changes, accumulates, or becomes harder to deny?

A theme is not yet an angle. `Missing home` names a topic. `I still give the taxi my old address` gives the singer a behavior and a conflict. The hook can name the behavior, contradict it, or reveal its cost. Do not impose this narrative method on a functional chant or instrumental.

## Choose An Entry Point

| Starting Material | First Useful Move | Protect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| User's lyric | Identify its strongest line and intended change before editing | Wording, POV, character, and scope they want kept |
| Title or phrase | Explore two meanings or situations the phrase could carry | Natural speech and memorable rhythm |
| Melody or demo | Map phrase entrances, stressed notes, rests, and repeated cells | Existing contour and timing where required |
| Beat or riff | Choose a cadence that leaves its accents audible | Pocket and signature figure |
| Story | Select the decisive scene; skip exposition that can be inferred | Cause, consequence, and listener orientation |
| Mood or image | Choose a recurring sensory motif and contrast | Atmosphere, including deliberate ambiguity |

For alternatives, offer different angles rather than synonyms for the same chorus. A refusal, a confession, and a practical request produce different songs even with the same title.

## Develop The Sections

| Section Job | What Changes | Common Failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First verse | Establish person, place, action, or voice | Backstory before a reason to care |
| Later verse | Add consequence, new evidence, changed behavior, or a time shift | Rephrasing the first verse's emotion |
| Pre-chorus | Increase pressure or make the hook feel necessary | A second verse with a different label |
| Chorus or refrain | Deliver the central recurring idea | Explaining the whole story again |
| Bridge or contrasting passage | Change perspective, tactics, harmony, or texture | New subplot too late in the song |
| Final return | Let accumulated context change how the hook is heard | A louder duplicate with no intended payoff |

Write a one-sentence job for each section before drafting a difficult song. If two jobs are identical, combine them or give the second a different function. A bridge can be instrumental contrast; it need not contain a revelation. A final chorus can keep every word unchanged and still gain meaning.

## Hook Design

Choose a primary hook carrier: words, melody, rhythm, riff, bass figure, response, or timbre. Let secondary hooks support it or occupy separate moments. Do not force a title into the lyric when the requested form or existing song works differently.

For a verbal hook:

1. Speak it naturally. Identify the stressed words and why this speaker would say them.
2. Find the central phrase; move setup away from its landing if it delays recognition.
3. Decide whether it opens the section, closes it, frames both ends, or recurs as an answer.
4. Leave enough rhythmic or melodic room to recognize it. Repetition, a rest, a contour, or a rhyme arrival can do this.
5. Test whether later context changes its meaning without adding explanatory lines.

Useful shapes include a command, an admission, a question, a double meaning, and a call answered by a short response. These are choices, not a quota. A dense chorus can work when its cadence or melody makes it recognizable; shorter words alone do not make a hook.

Original worked angle:

```text
Topic: leaving a relationship without a grand confrontation.
Angle: the singer asks for one ordinary object back.
Hook: Keep the photographs, give me back my coat.
Verse 1: standing in the hall, trying to sound practical.
Verse 2: realizing the coat is an excuse to stay another minute.
Final return: the same request now means choosing to leave.
```

The object carries the emotion. Do not explain the device in the sung lines.

## Prosody: Meaning, Stress, And Space

Prosody includes how lyric, melody, harmony, rhythm, and delivery support or deliberately oppose the song's intention. A cheerful groove with a bitter lyric can be intentional tension. Do not automatically make every musical layer express the same surface mood.

For a fixed melody, mark its phrase lengths, pickups, long notes, high notes, and rests. Match the stress pattern before matching the syllable count. Two eight-syllable lines can sing very differently.

- In ordinary speech, `I didn't SAY you should leave` and `I didn't say YOU should leave` make different claims. Choose the musical emphasis that serves the scene.
- Put a useful vowel on a sustained hook note; avoid making a consonant cluster carry the sustain. Do not require every ending to use the same open vowel.
- Leave breaths at thought boundaries where possible. A pause that splits a name or reverses the sense of a phrase may need rewriting.
- Use enjambment to carry thought across a bar or line when momentum needs it. A printed line is not a bar, breath, or guaranteed pause.
- A pickup, delayed entry, or anticipated accent can solve a stress collision. Do not shorten every line simply because it differs from its neighbor.
- For rap, check the pattern over beats and subdivisions. For multilingual lyrics, use the target language's stress, syllable, mora, or tonal needs rather than an English counting rule.

A compact revision can preserve the thought:

```text
Crowded: I keep on telling all our friends that I'm doing fine without you.
Tighter: I tell our friends I'm doing fine.
Response: Then leave before they ask me why.
```

The revision is useful if the phrase needs space. Keep the longer version if a fast conversational delivery is the point. Text review estimates singability; an existing melody or audio permits a stronger check.

## Rhyme Architecture

Choose where the listener expects a rhyme and whether to meet, delay, or break that expectation. Rhyme is an audible relationship, not matching spelling.

| Choice | Possible Use | Revision Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Couplet, AA BB | Quick closure, punchlines, direct hooks | Does every pair close too predictably? |
| Alternating, ABAB | Carry a thought across lines | Can the listener retain the longer rhyme distance? |
| Ballad-like, xAxA | Narrative room before a recurring landing | Do unrhymed lines still have a useful cadence? |
| Internal rhyme | Propulsion, rap flow, playful detail | Does it obscure the meaningful word? |
| Multisyllabic rhyme | Longer rhythmic identity | Do stresses match without distorting pronunciation? |
| Slant rhyme or assonance | Natural diction, looser connection | Does it sound intentional when spoken? |
| Unrhymed phrase or repeated response | Speech realism, chant, suspense | What supplies recognition instead? |

Build a small bank of meaningful rhyme options around the hook vowel, then write toward an action or thought. Do not choose a meaningless end word and invent a line merely to reach it. Perfect rhyme is not inferior to slant rhyme; forced syntax is the problem.

## Detail, Subtext, And Voice

Use details that change the listener's understanding: a coat left behind, a receipt kept, an address supplied by habit. A list of vivid objects can still be empty if none changes the situation. Prefer a character's action over an abstract claim when it conveys more.

Keep diction appropriate to the speaker. A plainspoken narrator should not become an ornate poet only to complete a rhyme. A deliberately surreal narrator can stay associative; consistency does not require literal exposition. Stock words are revision signals, not banned vocabulary.

Intentional POV or tense shifts need orientation. A remembered verse can lead into a present-tense refrain. Do not repair such a shift into uniform tense if it carries the song's meaning.

## Say The Plain Meaning First

When explaining a lyric, lead with the most direct supported reading. Distinguish what the text states from an interpretation. Offer alternate readings when the evidence supports them; do not invent an elaborate symbolic plot over a straightforward situation.

When writing, keep the working premise internal unless the user wants the plan. Some lyrics are intentionally ambiguous or driven by sound and image. State that rather than declaring them defective because they resist a single paraphrase.

## Preserve Or Transform

For the user's own draft, follow the requested edit depth. A line edit preserves unrelated material; a rewrite can replace structure and imagery. Do not automatically replace their title, hook, or voice just because they supplied a rough draft.

For a new song inspired by third-party lyrics, extract broad theme, function, and musical traits, then write fresh expression. Avoid reusing distinctive lines or closely following their sequence of images. For literal translation or singable adaptation, follow the requested mode in `multilingual.md`.

## Revision By Symptom

| Symptom | Diagnose | First Revision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Generic lyric | Topic without a speaker's specific angle | Give the singer a revealing action or contradiction |
| Pretty but static | Images do not affect one another | Add consequence or a deliberate accumulating pattern |
| Chorus does not land | Weak landing phrase, cadence, or contrast | Change one of those before adding a pre-chorus |
| Mouthful line | Too many stresses for the available phrase | Remove a clause, alter the entrance, or allocate more time |
| Verse 2 feels recycled | Same scene job and emotional information | Show what the first verse caused |
| Forced rhyme | End word controls the thought | Rewrite the whole couplet around the meaning |
| Constant intensity | No rest, register contrast, or change of texture | Remove one layer or create a gap |
| Technically polished but voiceless | Interchangeable diction and images | Restore a character-specific choice, even if less tidy |

Check identity and structure before polishing individual words. Read the revised lyric aloud, compare it with the user's constraints, then prepare Suno fields. Use `genre-songwriting.md` to avoid applying one family's solution to every genre.
