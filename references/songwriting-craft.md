# Songwriting Craft

Use this reference when writing or revising lyrics, hooks, sections, rhyme, meter, or narrative.

## Start With The Song Promise

Before drafting, identify one central promise:

- what the singer wants
- what they fear
- what they realize
- what they refuse
- what changes by the end

Every section should move that promise. If a section repeats the same emotional point without escalation, rewrite it.

## Section Jobs

- Verse: concrete situation, character, memory, conflict, image, or setup.
- Pre-Chorus: rising pressure, question, turn, or emotional lift.
- Chorus: title/hook, simplest emotional truth, repeatable language, stronger vowels, fewer details.
- Post-Chorus: short chant, melodic tag, or repeated phrase when the genre expects it.
- Bridge: new angle, confession, contradiction, time jump, or final realization.
- Outro: resolve, echo the hook, or leave one memorable image.

## Hook Strength

Ask:
- Can the chorus be understood without the verses?
- Does the title appear in or near the chorus?
- Is the hook short enough to remember after one listen?
- Does the final chorus gain meaning because of the bridge?
- Can the listener sing the emotional claim back?

Weak hook signs:
- too many abstract nouns
- no repeated phrase
- title does not appear
- every line explains instead of landing
- the chorus is just another verse

## Meter And Singability

- Keep line lengths and syllable load close within each section.
- Rewrite outlier lines that are noticeably longer than neighbors.
- Use commas for short pauses and periods for clear stops.
- Use repeated vowels for sustain when needed, for example `Goo-o-o-odbye`.
- Prefer compact syntax over cramming plot into one line.
- For rap, manage bar length and internal rhyme without making every line dense.
- For non-English lyrics, compare phrase length, natural stress, vowel endings, and breath points in the target language instead of forcing English syllable counts.

## Rhyme

Prefer near rhymes over forced perfect rhymes. Forced rhyme usually sounds more artificial than no rhyme.

Useful rhyme approaches:
- end rhyme for pop/country hooks
- internal rhyme for rap momentum
- vowel rhyme for emotional ballads
- slant rhyme for indie/folk naturalness
- repeated title phrase instead of rhyme when clarity matters more

## Vivid Detail

Use details that imply emotion:

- object: `your coffee ring on the windowsill`
- place: `the third-floor laundry room`
- habit: `you count the stairs when you lie`
- time: `2:17 on the oven clock`
- body state: `my jaw locked through the whole ride home`

Avoid replacing every abstract line with detail. Use detail where it sharpens the listener's picture.

## Anti-Generic Editing

Watch for stock words:

```text
neon, fire, flames, shadows, echoes, broken, fading, forever
```

These are not banned, but they should earn their place.

Substitutions:
- `fire in my heart` -> a physical action, object, or consequence
- `burning up` -> `hands shaking`, `throat dry`, `shirt stuck to my back`
- `neon nights` -> `corner diner sign`, `train platform light`, `exit 14 motel`
- `lost in the shadows` -> name the actual room, street, or silence
- `echoes of you` -> `your voicemail`, `your keys`, `your laugh through the wall`
- `broken dreams` -> abandoned plan, receipt, map, packed box, or missed call

## Reference Lyrics Transformation

When the user pastes lyrics as inspiration, theme, or rough draft, default to transformation instead of preservation.

Extract:
- theme
- speaker
- emotional arc
- motifs
- hook function

Then write:
- new title
- new hook phrase
- new imagery
- new scenes
- new section wording

Do not reuse distinctive lines, repeated hook phrases, or section wording unless the user explicitly asks to keep them.

If the reference may be copyrighted or from an existing song, avoid close paraphrase and do not continue it in the same wording or structure. Use a high-level thematic brief instead.

## Genre-Specific Lyric Notes

- Pop: immediate hook, short chorus phrases, strong title repetition.
- Country: plainspoken narrative, concrete nouns, conversational turns.
- Folk: image-driven verses, restrained chorus, natural rhyme.
- Rap: bar momentum, internal rhyme, punchlines, breath control.
- R&B: vowel flow, emotional phrasing, space for melisma.
- Rock: direct claims, physical images, shoutable chorus vowels.
- EDM: fewer words, repeatable phrases, build/drop tags.
- Musical theater: character intention and plot movement matter more than generic hook language.

## Revision Checklist

- Does each section have a distinct job?
- Is the chorus simpler and more repeatable than the verses?
- Are the longest lines singable?
- Is the hook emotionally clear?
- Are there 1-3 vivid human details?
- Are cliches replaced or justified?
- Is the source material transformed enough when reference lyrics were provided?
